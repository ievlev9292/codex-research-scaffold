# Editorial principles

Read for every invoked final pass. Judge a passage by what it communicates in context, not by presumed authorship.

## Diagnose function before surface form

Ask what each paragraph contributes: observation, method, comparison, interpretation, limitation, or a justified implication. A repeated setup-result-praise pattern deserves attention when its framing adds no information. Reorganize only where the source supplies the logical connection. Keep necessary transitions, section conventions, and summaries that actually synthesize.

Repetition and clustering can point to a weakness; they do not prove one. Formal vocabulary, passive voice, a three-part list, an em dash, a topic sentence, or a contrast may all be the best choice. Correct grammar and ordinary rhetorical constructions are not defects. Do not infer authorship, count signals, randomize sentence length, or replace one recurring template with another.

## Useful interventions

- **Generic importance:** delete ceremonial praise with no independent proposition. State the supplied result directly; do not invent a mechanism or application to replace praise.
- **Interpretive tails:** inspect clauses introduced by words such as "highlighting," "underscoring," or "demonstrating." Remove an empty evaluation, but retain an actual supported inference and its uncertainty.
- **Promotion:** replace applause with the factual description already present. Preserve persuasive purpose where the genre calls for it.
- **Attribution:** use a specific source only if supplied and clearly linked to the claim. Retain exact citation tokens. An unidentified "experts agree" is not permission to name an expert or certify consensus.
- **Inflation:** simplify wording only when its technical meaning and scope remain identical. "Statistically significant," "robust estimator," and "critical temperature" carry domain meanings.
- **Redundancy:** remove repeated framing or conclusions that add nothing. Repeat the same technical term when synonyms could imply distinct entities.
- **Invented-sounding jargon:** inspect definitions and authorized context. If a phrase appears locally coined or undefined, identify it briefly and retain it if its meaning is uncertain. Do not declare it absent from the discipline without evidence, start an unsolicited literature search, or replace it with a familiar but inequivalent term.

## Deletion versus scientific revision

Separate detachable evaluation ("an exciting result") from a proposition ("the first observation," "proves causality," "improves sensitivity by 20%"). A proposition stays protected even if it seems exaggerated or poorly supported. Preserve it and briefly flag the support or ambiguity; factual correction requires a separate, informed scientific decision. The same applies to author commitments and judgments in reviewer responses.

A decoration can be removed without a note when it adds no content. If uncertain whether a phrase carries an intended claim, retain it and flag it. Do not silently change "proves" to "suggests," "all" to "some," an association to a cause, a future plan to a completed action, or a single attribution to consensus. Do not add a hedge to make an invented inference seem acceptable.

## Fidelity and stopping

Compare propositions, qualifications, scope, attribution, and connections in both directions: every retained claim must have a source counterpart, and every substantive source claim must survive. Check connective words such as "therefore," "after," "only," and "because" as carefully as numbers. Confirm exact protected spans and their attachment to the surrounding claims.

When further edits offer only cosmetic variation or risk changing meaning, stop. No change is a valid result. This pass does not validate the science, audit references externally, clean invisible characters, or make claims about how the text was authored.
